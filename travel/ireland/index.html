Ireland with kids: when to go, routes, costs and the car hire insurance catch

The practical stuff for a family trip to Ireland: what the weather is really like, how long the drives take, what's worth booking ahead, and the rental car insurance surprise that catches a lot of Americans at the desk. Facts checked October 2026, with sources at the end.

1. Before you book: passports, and the Northern Ireland catch

  • The Republic of Ireland: US citizens don't need a visa for a tourist trip of up to 90 days. Your passport has to be valid for the whole stay.
  • Northern Ireland (Belfast, the Giant's Causeway) is part of the UK, not the Republic of Ireland. There's no passport check on the land border, but you still need a UK ETA: £20 per person, babies included, applied for on GOV.UK or the official UK ETA app. Northern Ireland also uses pounds, not euros.
  • Kids' passports: US passports for children under 16 are only valid for 5 years, so check expiry dates against your return date now.

A nice bonus on the way home: Dublin and Shannon airports have US preclearance, so you clear US immigration and customs before you board and land in the US like a domestic flight.

2. When to go

Ireland is mild all year, rarely very hot or very cold, and it can rain in any month. The west gets a lot more rain than Dublin. What changes most is daylight, crowds and prices.

  • May, June and September are our pick with kids: long days, drier weather than autumn, and fewer crowds than high summer.
  • July and August are the busiest and priciest months for rooms and cars, so book early.
  • Winter is usually cheaper and quiet, but days are short and some smaller attractions on the west coast keep reduced hours. Plan one or two things a day.

3. Getting around: driving on the left and real drive times

A car is the easiest way to see the west with kids. In Ireland you drive on the left, distances are in kilometres and speed limits in km/h, and many rural roads are narrow and twisty with stone walls right at the edge.   • Shortcut from Clare to Kerry: the Killimer–Tarbert car ferry across the Shannon estuary takes about 20 minutes and saves the long drive round through Limerick.
  • Car seats: children must use a child seat or booster until they're 150 cm tall or 36 kg. Request them when you book, or bring your own.
  • Automatics are less common than manuals, so book early if you need one.
  • M50 toll: the Dublin ring road has a camera toll between junctions 6 and 7. Pay at eflow.ie by 8pm the next day, unless your rental company handles it.
  • Going north? Tell the rental company you'll drive into Northern Ireland and ask if there's a cross-border fee.

4. The rental car insurance catch (read this before you book)

Lots of US credit cards include rental car damage cover, and plenty of people plan to rely on it. Ireland is one of the places where that often doesn't work, and where it does, the rental desk can still make it awkward:

  • Many Visa cards don't cover Ireland at all. Visa's standard Auto Rental Collision Damage Waiver, used by many Visa Signature cards, excludes the Republic of Ireland, Northern Ireland, Israel and Jamaica.
  • Some premium cards do. Chase's Visa Infinite guide to benefits (the one for cards like Sapphire Reserve) says coverage is available worldwide, and it's primary.
  • American Express: the standard Car Rental Loss and Damage Insurance on most Amex cards excludes Australia, Italy and New Zealand but not Ireland. It's secondary cover, and you have to pay for the whole rental with the card and decline the desk's collision damage waiver. Amex's paid add-on, Premium Car Rental Protection, does exclude Ireland.
  • At the desk: Hertz's Dublin terms, for example, say that if you rely on a credit card you need documents showing the cover applies in the Republic of Ireland. They also add a €10.22-a-day Supplementary Liability Waiver and put a €5,000 hold on your card if you decline their CDW.

What that means for you: call the number on your card, ask "does my rental car cover apply in the Republic of Ireland (and Northern Ireland, if you're going)?", and get a letter of coverage in writing. Check you have room on the card for a large hold. If your card doesn't cover Ireland, budget for the rental company's CDW (collision damage waiver), or for "excess" cover that lowers the amount you'd owe after damage. It can add a lot to the daily rate, so get the full quote before comparing cars on price.

5. Two sample routes

7 nights: Dublin and the west

Dublin (2 nights, no car needed) → pick up the car and drive to Galway (2 nights, with a day for the Burren and the Cliffs of Moher) → ferry across the Shannon to Killarney (2 nights, with a day on the Dingle Peninsula) → Shannon Airport (1 night nearby, or fly the same day). Flying home from Shannon saves the long drive back to Dublin.

10 nights: Dublin, the south and the Wild Atlantic Way

Dublin (3 nights) → Kilkenny (1 night: a castle and an easy medieval town for little legs) → Killarney (2 nights, Ring of Kerry or Killarney National Park) → Dingle (1 night) → ferry to Clare and the Cliffs of Moher → Galway (2 nights) → Dublin (1 night before the flight). The Wild Atlantic Way is the signed coastal route along the whole west coast, and this trip follows one piece of it, from Dingle up to Galway.

Adding the Giant's Causeway

The Giant's Causeway is in Northern Ireland, about three hours' mapped drive from Dublin, so it's a long day trip with kids. It works better as two nights near Belfast or on the Causeway Coast. Remember it's the UK: everyone needs a UK ETA, prices are in pounds, and tell your rental company you're crossing the border.

Rather skip the driving for a day? Coach day trips from Dublin to the Cliffs of Moher, Galway or the Giant's Causeway are listed on GetYourGuide and Klook. With young kids, check how many hours you're actually on the coach.

6. What things cost (and the passes worth knowing)

Flights, rooms and the car will be most of your budget, and they depend so much on dates that we won't guess at them. Get real quotes. Some fixed costs worth knowing:

  • OPW Heritage Card: one year of free entry to all state-run OPW heritage sites (places like Dublin Castle, Kilkenny Castle and the Rock of Cashel). Adult €40, family €90 (two adults and up to five children aged 12 to 18). Children under 12 get in free at these sites anyway. It's valid for a year from first use, and you can buy it at the first site you visit.
  • Cliffs of Moher: 2026 gate prices are €15 per adult or €30 for a family, and online tickets are cheaper, especially before 11am or after 4pm. Children under 12 get in free with a parent (up to 4 per adult).
  • Book of Kells, Dublin: timed tickets often sell out in summer, so book ahead. Tiqets sells Book of Kells tickets, and so does Trinity College's own site, so compare.
  • UK ETA if you're going to Northern Ireland: £20 per person.
  • Car rental insurance: see section 4. It's the cost people most often leave out.
  • Phone data: US plans can charge a lot to roam. A travel eSIM such as Airalo's Ireland eSIM is an easy fix. If you're crossing into Northern Ireland, check that the plan covers the UK too, or pick a Europe-wide plan.

7. Packing for Irish weather

  • Layers, not a big coat. Even in summer, averages sit in the high 50s °F, and the weather changes fast on the coast.
  • A proper waterproof jacket for everyone, kids included, plus waterproof shoes or boots. The Cliffs of Moher and Dingle can be very windy.
  • A change of clothes in the car for wet or muddy kids.
  • Plug adapters: Ireland and Northern Ireland both use the UK-style three-pin plug (type G).

8. Tips for traveling Ireland with kids

  • Keep most driving days under two to three hours of mapped time, and don't stack long days back to back.
  • Base yourself for two nights or more where you can, so there are days when you don't pack the car.
  • Have a rain plan in each place: museums, castles, aquariums and caves are easy wins when the weather turns.
  • Cliff edges: stay on the marked paths at the Cliffs of Moher and other coastal spots, and keep small kids' hands held. Wind gusts are strong.
  • Jet lag: Ireland is usually 5 hours ahead of US Eastern time. Get outside in daylight on day one and keep the first day light.
  • Pubs: lots of pubs serve food and welcome families during the day and early evening. Policies on children later at night vary, so ask.

9. What to book when

  • 6 to 9 months out: rooms in Dingle, Killarney and Galway for July and August. Check Booking.com or local B&Bs.
  • 4 to 6 months out: flights and the rental car (especially an automatic), after you've confirmed your card's insurance situation.
  • 1 to 2 months out: timed tickets for the Book of Kells and the Cliffs of Moher, and any tours.
  • Once flights are booked: passport check, a UK ETA if you're going north, and travel insurance.
  • The week before: your card's letter of coverage, offline maps, and the eSIM installed.

Questions

Do US citizens need a visa for Ireland?

No. US citizens can visit the Republic of Ireland as tourists for up to 90 days without a visa, and the passport needs to be valid for the whole stay. Northern Ireland is different: it's part of the UK, so you need a UK ETA (£20 per person) to visit, even if you just drive across the border.

Does my credit card cover a rental car in Ireland?

It depends on the card, so check its guide to benefits before you book. The standard Visa benefit used by many Visa Signature cards excludes the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland. Chase's Visa Infinite guide covers rentals worldwide. American Express's standard car rental cover leaves out Australia, Italy and New Zealand but not Ireland, though it's secondary, and Amex's paid Premium Car Rental Protection does exclude Ireland. Even with cover, Irish rental desks can ask for proof, a big card hold, or an extra daily charge.

How many days do you need in Ireland with kids?

Seven nights is enough for Dublin and one region of the west, like Galway and the Cliffs of Moher, or Kerry. With ten nights you can do Dublin, Kerry and the Galway area without long drives every day. Northern Ireland and the Giant's Causeway really need two extra nights.

Is the OPW Heritage Card worth it for a family?

If you'll visit several state-run sites, often yes. The family card is €90 for two adults and up to five children aged 12 to 18, and children under 12 already get in free at OPW sites. With younger kids only, compare a couple of adult cards (€40 each) with the individual tickets you'd buy.

Do kids need car seats in Ireland?

Yes. Irish law says children must use a suitable child seat or booster until they're 150 cm tall or weigh 36 kg. Request seats when you book the car, or bring your own.

Do I pay the M50 toll around Dublin?

If you drive the stretch of the M50 between junctions 6 and 7, yes. There's no booth: cameras read the plate, and you pay online at eflow.ie by 8pm the next day. Ask the rental company first, because some add tolls to your bill.
