Why a daily number works better than a trip total

Once you're away, a trip total is hard to keep track of. A daily number is easy: if lunch and the museum come in under it, you've got a little extra for tomorrow. If one day runs over, you know to keep the next one simple.

What counts as "already paid"

Anything booked and paid before you go: flights, rooms, a rental car paid in advance, tickets you bought online. Don't include deposits with a balance due on arrival; leave that balance in the daily money, or add it to "already paid" once you've set it aside.

Kids as a share of an adult

Families rarely spend the same on every person. Counting each child as half an adult is a common rule of thumb, but you know your kids: teenagers can out-eat their parents, and babies cost almost nothing on the ground (apart from the diapers you packed).

Questions

How do I work out a daily budget for a trip?

Start with the total you're willing to spend, take off everything already paid (flights, rooms, tours booked ahead) and hold back a little for surprises. Divide what's left by the number of days. That's your day-to-day money for food, transport, tickets and treats.

How should I split a travel budget between adults and kids?

Kids usually cost less per day than adults (smaller meals, child tickets), so the calculator lets you count each child as a share of an adult: 50% is a sensible start. Set it to 100% if your kids eat and pay like grown-ups, or 0% if they're babies.

Which exchange rate should I use?

Use today's rate from your bank or a currency site as "how much local money one unit of your home money buys". Card and ATM rates are often a little worse, so rounding the rate down slightly gives you a safer number.

Is this a daily budget in pounds or dollars?

Both. Every amount is shown in your home currency and in the local currency at the rate you enter, so you can check prices on the spot.

Is my information saved anywhere?

No. The math runs in your browser and nothing you type is sent anywhere.
